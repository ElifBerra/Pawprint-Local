# Common Symptoms and What They Suggest

This document describes frequently seen symptoms and the general reasoning
around them. It is a guide to whether something needs attention, not a
diagnostic tool. Diagnosis requires examination and usually testing.

## Vomiting

A single episode in an otherwise bright, active animal that is still drinking
is usually not alarming. Withhold food for six to eight hours, keep water
available, and reintroduce small bland meals.

Seek care when vomiting repeats more than two or three times in a day,
continues beyond twenty-four hours, contains blood or material resembling
coffee grounds, or occurs alongside lethargy, a painful abdomen, or an inability
to keep water down. Repeated unproductive retching in a dog with a swollen
abdomen is an emergency.

## Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea lasting a day or two in an adult animal that is otherwise well often
follows a dietary change or scavenging. Feed a bland diet of boiled chicken and
rice or a prescription intestinal food for two to three days.

Escalate when diarrhoea persists beyond forty-eight hours, contains fresh blood
or is black and tarry, or appears in a puppy or kitten. Young animals dehydrate
quickly and parvovirus is a genuine risk in the undervaccinated.

## Reduced appetite

A dog skipping one meal is common. A cat that eats nothing for more than
twenty-four hours is a different matter: cats that stop eating can develop
hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver condition, and this risk is higher in
overweight cats.

## Coughing

An occasional cough after excitement or pulling on a lead is usually
mechanical. A dry, honking cough often points to kennel cough or tracheal
irritation. A moist cough, coughing that worsens at night or when lying down,
or coughing with laboured breathing suggests heart or lung disease and needs
prompt assessment. Cats do not typically cough from infection; a coughing cat is
more often asthmatic.

## Lameness

Mild limping after unusual exercise may settle with two days of rest. Seek care
for non-weight-bearing lameness, obvious swelling, a limb held at an abnormal
angle, or lameness that persists past a few days. Sudden hind limb weakness in
a cat, especially with pain and cold paws, can indicate a blood clot and is an
emergency.

## Scratching and skin changes

Persistent scratching, licking, or chewing usually traces back to parasites,
allergy, or infection. Look for fleas and flea dirt at the base of the tail
first. Seasonal itch often reflects environmental allergy; year-round itch with
ear infections and paw licking more often suggests food sensitivity. Hair loss,
scabbing, an unpleasant odour, or open sores from self-trauma warrant
examination.

## Increased thirst and urination

Drinking noticeably more than usual is a meaningful signal in both species and
should not be attributed to warm weather without checking. It is a common early
sign of kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, and hormonal disorders. Measuring
water intake over twenty-four hours before the appointment gives the clinic
useful information.

## Changes in behaviour

Hiding, unusual aggression when handled, restlessness, or reluctance to jump
often indicate pain rather than a behavioural problem. Cats in particular mask
discomfort, and a change in routine is sometimes the only visible sign.
