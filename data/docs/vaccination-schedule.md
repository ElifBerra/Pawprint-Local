# Vaccination Schedules for Dogs and Cats

Vaccination protects pets against diseases that are common, serious, and in
several cases fatal. Schedules differ by species, age, local disease risk, and
legal requirements, so the plan below is a general reference rather than a
prescription. A veterinarian sets the final schedule for an individual animal.

## Core vaccines for dogs

Core vaccines are recommended for every dog regardless of lifestyle.

**Distemper, adenovirus, parainfluenza, and parvovirus (DHPP)** are usually
given as a single combination injection. Puppies receive their first dose at
six to eight weeks of age, then boosters every two to four weeks until they are
at least sixteen weeks old. The final dose must fall after sixteen weeks
because antibodies inherited from the mother can block the vaccine before then.
A booster follows one year later, and after that most dogs are revaccinated
every three years.

**Rabies** is legally required in most countries. The first dose is given at
twelve to sixteen weeks, followed by a booster after one year. Subsequent
boosters are given every one to three years depending on local law and the
vaccine product used.

## Non-core vaccines for dogs

These depend on where the dog lives and what it does. **Bordetella**
(kennel cough) is advised for dogs that board, attend daycare, visit groomers,
or go to dog parks; protection lasts roughly six to twelve months.
**Leptospirosis** matters for dogs exposed to standing water, wildlife, or
rural environments, and requires an annual booster. **Lyme disease** vaccination
is considered in regions with high tick density.

## Core vaccines for cats

**Feline viral rhinotracheitis, calicivirus, and panleukopenia (FVRCP)** is the
core combination. Kittens start at six to eight weeks and receive boosters
every three to four weeks until sixteen weeks of age. A booster follows at one
year, then every three years for most adult cats.

**Rabies** follows the same pattern as in dogs: first dose at twelve to sixteen
weeks, booster at one year, then every one to three years.

## Non-core vaccines for cats

**Feline leukemia virus (FeLV)** vaccination is recommended for cats that go
outdoors or live with FeLV-positive cats. Kittens receive two doses three to
four weeks apart. Because outdoor risk changes over a cat's life, the need for
continued boosters should be reviewed annually.

## After vaccination

Mild reactions are common and usually resolve within a day or two: reduced
appetite, low energy, mild fever, or tenderness at the injection site. These do
not normally require treatment.

Signs that need immediate veterinary attention include swelling of the face or
muzzle, hives, repeated vomiting, difficulty breathing, or collapse. These
suggest an allergic reaction and typically appear within minutes to a few hours
of the injection.

## Keeping records

Keep a written or digital record of every vaccine, including the date, product
name, and the date the next dose is due. Boarding facilities, groomers, and
international travel authorities all require proof of vaccination, and
reconstructing a lost history usually means starting a series over.
