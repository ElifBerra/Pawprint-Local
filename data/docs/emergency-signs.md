# Emergency Warning Signs

Some situations cannot wait for a routine appointment. This document lists
signs that warrant contacting an emergency veterinary clinic immediately. When
in doubt, calling a clinic costs nothing and the staff can tell you whether to
come in.

## Breathing

Any difficulty breathing is an emergency. Look for open-mouth breathing in
cats, which is never normal at rest; a stretched-out neck and elbows held away
from the body; exaggerated abdominal effort with each breath; or gums and
tongue that appear blue, grey, or very pale. Continuous coughing that prevents
rest also needs same-day attention.

## Bloating and unproductive retching

A dog with a swollen, firm abdomen that repeatedly tries to vomit without
bringing anything up may have gastric dilatation-volvulus, in which the stomach
fills with gas and twists. This is rapidly fatal without surgery. Deep-chested
breeds are at highest risk. Do not wait to see whether it settles.

## Urinary blockage

A cat straining in the litter box, producing little or no urine, crying, or
repeatedly licking the genital area may have a urethral obstruction. This is
most common in male cats and becomes life-threatening within roughly
twenty-four hours. It is frequently mistaken for constipation.

## Seizures

A single seizure lasting under two minutes in a pet with known epilepsy may not
require an emergency visit, but any first seizure, a seizure lasting longer
than three minutes, or two or more seizures in a day does. During a seizure,
move furniture away, do not put anything in the mouth, dim the lights, and time
the episode.

## Suspected poisoning

Contact a clinic or a pet poison helpline immediately if the animal may have
eaten chocolate, xylitol (birch sugar, found in sugar-free gum and some peanut
butters), grapes or raisins, onions or garlic, lilies (extremely toxic to
cats), antifreeze, rodenticide, or any human medication. Bring the packaging.
Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional tells you to; with
corrosive substances it causes further damage.

## Trauma

After a fall from height, a road accident, or an attack by another animal, have
the pet examined even if it appears to be walking normally. Internal bleeding,
a ruptured bladder, and diaphragmatic tears can all be silent for hours.

## Body temperature

Heatstroke presents as heavy panting, bright red gums, drooling, unsteadiness,
vomiting, and eventually collapse. Move the animal to shade, wet the fur with
cool, not ice-cold, water, and travel to a clinic. Hypothermia in a small or
elderly animal, with shivering that stops and a body temperature below normal,
is equally urgent.

## Bleeding and eyes

Bleeding that does not slow after five minutes of firm pressure requires
immediate care. Eye injuries also count as emergencies: squinting, a visibly
cloudy or bulging eye, or any penetrating injury can cost the animal its sight
within a day.

## Collapse and pale gums

Sudden weakness, collapse, or gums that are white, grey, or brick red indicate a
circulatory problem. Healthy gums are pink and moist, and colour returns within
two seconds after pressing with a fingertip.
