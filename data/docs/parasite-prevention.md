# Parasite Prevention

## Fleas

Fleas are the most common external parasite in both dogs and cats. Adult fleas
on the animal represent only a small fraction of the population; the majority
exists as eggs, larvae, and pupae in carpets, bedding, and floor cracks. This is
why treating the pet alone often fails and the problem appears to return.

Signs include scratching, small dark specks in the coat that turn reddish-brown
when placed on damp paper, hair loss around the tail base, and small scabs
along the back. Some animals develop flea allergy dermatitis, in which a single
bite triggers intense itching out of proportion to the number of fleas present.

Effective control means treating every pet in the household simultaneously,
continuing monthly through the entire year in heated homes, and washing bedding
at sixty degrees. Environmental treatment may be needed for established
infestations. Fleas also transmit tapeworm, so a flea problem usually warrants
deworming as well.

## Ticks

Ticks attach to feed and transmit disease during that process. Risk is highest
in grassland, woodland, and areas with deer or livestock, though urban parks are
not exempt.

Check the animal after walks, concentrating on the head, ears, neck, armpits,
and between the toes. Remove ticks with a purpose-made tick hook, twisting
steadily rather than pulling straight out, and avoid crushing the body.
Petroleum jelly, alcohol, and heat all increase the chance the tick regurgitates
into the wound and should not be used.

Diseases transmitted by ticks include Lyme disease, babesiosis, ehrlichiosis,
and anaplasmosis. Transmission generally requires the tick to remain attached
for a number of hours, so prompt removal matters.

## Intestinal worms

**Roundworms** are extremely common in puppies and kittens, often passed from
the mother before birth or through milk. They can appear in vomit or faeces
looking like strands of spaghetti. **Hookworms** attach to the intestinal wall
and cause anaemia, which is dangerous in young animals. **Tapeworms** appear as
rice-like segments around the anus or in bedding and are acquired from fleas or
from hunting. **Whipworms** cause chronic large intestinal diarrhoea and are
difficult to eliminate from a contaminated environment.

Puppies and kittens are typically wormed every two weeks from two weeks of age
until twelve weeks, then monthly until six months. Adults are usually treated
every three months, more often if they hunt, scavenge, or live with young
children. Several intestinal worms are transmissible to humans, particularly
children, which is the main reason routine deworming is advised even in animals
showing no symptoms.

## Heartworm

Heartworm is transmitted by mosquitoes and is endemic in many warm regions. The
adult worms live in the heart and pulmonary arteries, and treatment of an
established infection is expensive and carries real risk. Monthly preventive
medication is far safer than treatment. Dogs should be tested before starting
prevention, since giving preventives to an already infected animal can cause a
severe reaction.

## Choosing products

Products differ substantially in what they cover, and no single product covers
everything. Some flea treatments do not cover ticks, and most flea and tick
products do not cover intestinal worms. Permethrin, found in some dog
treatments, is highly toxic to cats, so products must never be shared between
species. Dosing follows body weight, and an underdosed treatment fails while an
overdosed one can harm.
