# Dental Care and Grooming

## Why dental disease matters

Periodontal disease is the most commonly diagnosed condition in adult dogs and
cats. Plaque forms on the tooth surface within hours of cleaning and hardens
into tartar within a few days. Tartar sits below the gum line and drives
inflammation that eventually destroys the tissue holding the tooth in place.

Beyond tooth loss, chronic oral infection places a continuous burden on the
body, and associations have been described with kidney, liver, and heart
disease. Animals rarely stop eating because of dental pain; they adapt, which
is why the problem is often advanced by the time an owner notices.

## Signs of dental problems

Bad breath is the earliest and most reliable sign. Others include red or
swollen gums, visible brown deposits along the gum line, drooling, chewing on
one side, dropping food, pawing at the mouth, and reluctance to accept hard
food or toys. Bleeding when the gums are touched is not normal.

## Home dental care

Brushing is the most effective home measure. Use a toothpaste made for animals,
since human toothpaste contains fluoride and often xylitol, both harmful if
swallowed. Introduce it gradually: let the animal taste the paste for a few
days, then touch the brush to the teeth briefly, and build up to a short
brushing session. Daily brushing is meaningfully better than weekly, because
plaque mineralises quickly.

Dental diets, chews, and water additives help but do not replace brushing.
Hard objects such as bones, antlers, and hooves are a common cause of fractured
teeth and are best avoided.

Professional cleaning requires general anaesthesia, because assessing and
cleaning below the gum line is not possible in a conscious animal.
Anaesthesia-free dental cleaning addresses only the visible surface and gives a
misleading impression of health.

## Coat care

Brushing frequency depends on coat type. Short smooth coats need weekly
attention. Double coats need brushing several times a week, and daily during
seasonal shedding. Long or curly coats mat easily and generally need daily
attention.

Mats are more than cosmetic. They pull on the skin, trap moisture, and hide
sores and parasites. Tight mats close to the skin should be clipped out by a
groomer or veterinary team rather than cut with scissors, as the skin lifts into
the mat and is easily cut.

Most cats groom themselves adequately, but long-haired cats, overweight cats
that cannot reach their back, and elderly cats with joint pain need help.

## Bathing

Dogs need bathing only when dirty or when a medicated wash is prescribed.
Frequent bathing strips the coat and irritates the skin. Use a shampoo
formulated for dogs; human shampoo has the wrong pH. Cats rarely need bathing
at all.

## Nails and ears

Nails should be trimmed when they touch the ground as the animal stands. The
quick, the pink vascular core, is visible in pale nails and must be avoided; in
dark nails, take small amounts at a time. Overgrown nails alter posture and can
grow into the pad. Dewclaws do not contact the ground and are frequently
missed.

Ears should be cleaned only when there is visible debris, using a product made
for the purpose. Cotton buds push material deeper. A dark, waxy, foul-smelling
discharge, head shaking, or a painful ear suggests infection and needs
examination rather than cleaning, since some treatments are unsafe if the
eardrum is damaged.
