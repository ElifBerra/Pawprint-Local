# Nutrition and Feeding

## How much to feed

Feeding guidelines printed on packaging are averages for an unneutered adult at
a healthy weight and typically overestimate the needs of a neutered, indoor, or
older animal. Portion size should be adjusted based on body condition rather
than followed literally.

Body condition is assessed by touch. In an animal at a healthy weight the ribs
can be felt easily under a thin layer of fat without pressing hard, the waist is
visible from above, and the abdomen tucks up when viewed from the side. If the
ribs cannot be felt, reduce the daily amount by roughly ten percent and reassess
in a month.

## Feeding frequency

Puppies under four months need three to four meals a day because they cannot
store enough energy between larger meals. From four to twelve months, two to
three meals a day is appropriate. Adult dogs do well on two meals a day.

Cats are naturally frequent, small-volume eaters. Several small meals suit them
better than one large one, and food puzzles or slow feeders help indoor cats
spend energy on eating rather than eating out of boredom.

## Transitioning between foods

Changing food abruptly commonly causes diarrhoea. Transition over seven to ten
days: for the first two days offer roughly a quarter new food, then half, then
three quarters, then the new food alone. If loose stool appears, hold at the
current ratio for a few extra days instead of pushing forward.

## Water

Fresh water should always be available. Cats fed exclusively dry food drink
noticeably less total water than those on wet food, which is relevant because
low urine volume contributes to urinary tract disease. Many cats prefer moving
water and drink more from a fountain. Sudden, marked increases in thirst are
not a dietary matter and should be investigated, as they can indicate kidney
disease, diabetes, or hyperadrenocorticism.

## Foods that are dangerous

Several common human foods are toxic to pets. **Chocolate** contains
theobromine, and dark chocolate carries far more of it than milk chocolate.
**Xylitol**, also sold as birch sugar, appears in sugar-free gum, sweets, and
some peanut butters, and causes a sudden collapse in blood sugar in dogs.
**Grapes and raisins** can cause kidney failure at doses that vary
unpredictably between individuals. **Onions, garlic, leeks, and chives** damage
red blood cells, whether raw, cooked, or powdered. **Macadamia nuts**,
**alcohol**, and **raw yeast dough** are also unsafe.

Cooked bones splinter and should never be given. Fatty scraps can trigger
pancreatitis, particularly in small breeds.

## Treats

Treats should stay under roughly ten percent of daily calories. Beyond that
they unbalance a complete diet. Many vegetables make useful low-calorie treats
for dogs, including carrot, green beans, and cucumber.

## Life stage diets

Growth formulas are more calorie- and mineral-dense than adult food. Large
breed puppies need a formula designed specifically for them, because excess
calcium and rapid growth increase the risk of joint disease. Senior diets
generally reduce phosphorus and add joint support, but age alone is not a
reason to switch; the decision should follow a health assessment.
